Lesson 06 · Windowed prefill

The roof that didn't move

Lesson 05 ended with a prediction: block skipping cuts loads and compute by different factors, so it should move the kernel from one roof to the other. This lesson works out the arithmetic. The prediction is wrong — and the reason it is wrong is worth more than the roofline itself.

You can now write the kernel. What you cannot yet do is the thing MISSION.md asks for last: read a roofline and state which roof binds, with the arithmetic to back it. That is two hardware constants, one ratio, and a habit — and the habit is the part that transfers.

1 · Two roofs and a ridge

A v5e can do at most so much arithmetic per second, and move at most so many bytes per second. Neither limit knows about the other. Plot attainable rate against arithmetic intensity — FLOPs performed per byte of HBM traffic — and the two limits become two lines:

attainable FLOP/s = min( peak FLOP/s ,   intensity × peak bandwidth )

A horizontal roof you cannot climb over, and a sloped roof whose slope is the bandwidth. They meet at the ridge point, at intensity = peak FLOP/s ÷ peak bandwidth. Left of the ridge, bytes decide; right of it, arithmetic does. That is the whole model: Williams, Waterman & Patterson, 2008.

One roof, and two ways to count a FLOP

There is one horizontal roof on a v5e, always: 197 TFLOP/s of bf16. Asking for more numerical precision does not lower it — the chip has no fp32 matmul unit, so it emulates one with three or six bf16 passes, and the extra work lands in the FLOP count, moving your point right and up along the same two lines. Every FLOP figure in this lesson is bf16, hence one pass, so nothing here needs adjusting for it; the distinction matters the moment you report an achieved rate at HIGHEST. See ADR-0004.

The constants, and the disagreement in them

Peak bf16 is not in dispute: 197 TFLOP/s, stated identically by Cloud TPU v5e and by JAX's own hardware table. Bandwidth is another matter. Three sources, three numbers:

They are not the same number in different clothes — 800 GiBps is 859e9 B/s, which is 4.8% above 820e9. Resolve it rather than averaging it. The same cloud table writes HBM capacity as “16 GB” where JAX's table records 17,200,000,000 bytes — that is 16 GiB. So the page's GB/GiB labelling is demonstrably loose, and 820e9 B/s is the number to work with. Carry the other two as a boundary, not as a correction.

A primary source you can query offline

The v5e row of JAX's hardware table is runnable, which is worth more than a doc page you have to trust:

from jax._src.pallas.mosaic import tpu_info as ti
ti.get_tpu_info_for_chip(ti.ChipVersion.TPU_V5E, 1)

It returns mem_bw_bytes_per_second=820000000000, bf16_ops_per_second=197000000000000, vmem_capacity_bytes=134217728 (the 128 MiB lesson 04 argued for), smem_capacity_bytes=1048576, and num_mxus=4 with mxu_column_size=128. Both roofs, the VMEM ceiling and the MXU geometry, from one call, on a laptop with no chip in it.

num_mxus=4 is the fine print on the compute roof. 197 TFLOP/s is four 128×128 MXUs all fed, every cycle. Lesson 04's block-shape rule is that requirement wearing a different hat: a block that does not fill a 128-wide tile does not reach the roof, so the roof stops being a bound you are chasing and becomes one you cannot see.

2 · Intensity belongs to the schedule, not the chip

The ridge is a fact about a v5e. Intensity is a fact about your kernel — and not even about its algorithm, but about the schedule you gave it. Same maths, different BlockSpecs, different intensity. That is why this is the last lesson rather than the first: you needed a real grid before the x-axis meant anything.

The same goes for precision, and it moves the mark further than any BlockSpec will: v5e has no fp32 matmul unit, so an fp32-precision matmul is emulated with three or six bf16 passes, and counting those passes multiplies the intensity by three or six over bytes that never moved. Intensity is a fact about a kernel as executed — schedule and precision both.

Counting it for one local layer of Gemma 3 1B, bf16, on splash_attention's grid (head, q_block, kv_block): 

FLOPs = 4 · pairs · head_dim          # pairs at BLOCK granularity — the kernel computes whole blocks
bytes = (copies issued) · block · head_dim · 2

Every DMA count below comes from lesson 03's elision rule, executed — the widget calls PipelineGrid.trace, the same function that drew lesson 03's picture and counted lesson 05's traffic. Q, K, V and O blocks are all (block × head_dim), so one DMA is one DMA whichever operand it belongs to.

Count all four operands, not just K

Lesson 05 counted K DMAs, because K was what block skipping was doing something to. A roofline cannot be that selective: Q and O are read and written once each no matter what the mask says, and at a shrunk grid they are half the total traffic. This is where lesson 05's prediction goes wrong, and it goes wrong before any hardware is involved.

3 · Put the kernel on the chart

Defaults are Gemma 3 1B's real geometry: N = 32768, W = 512, B = 512. Hover either mark for its numbers; the bandwidth control changes which published constant the ridge is drawn from.

The y-axis is a rate, so a mark's height says nothing about how long the kernel runs. The time floor is in the table, and it is the number you actually want. Note also that the ridge band — the whole 4.9% spread between the three published bandwidths — is about three pixels wide. That is the answer to “which source should I use”.

4 · The prediction, tested

At the defaults, block skipping does this:

FLOPsHBM bytesintensitytime floor
dense grid4.40 TFLOP8.12 GiB504.122.3 ms
shrunk grid + table136 GFLOP256 MiB508.0692 µs
factor32.25×32.50×1.008×32.25×
The result

Block skipping is regime-preserving by construction. A dead block takes its load and its compute away together — that is what “dead” means — so the numerator and the denominator of the intensity fall by the same factor and the ratio survives. Both configurations are compute-bound by 2.1×. They are the same point on the chart, 32× apart in time. The kernel moved along the roof, not between roofs.

So where did 4096 → 64 loads against 4096 → 127 steps go? It was real — K traffic really does fall 64× while compute falls 32×. But K is a quarter of the operands and, once the grid is shrunk, only a quarter of the bytes. Q and O do not move at all. Fold them in and 64× and 32× average back to 32.5×.

Which is the useful reading

Not “the roofline had nothing to say”. It said something specific and load-bearing: the 32× is all of the win, and there is no second win hiding in a regime change. Before you had the arithmetic, “it might also move which roof binds” was a live hypothesis worth chasing. Now it is closed, in one ratio, and you can stop.

5 · So what does move it

Answer before you scroll

You may change exactly one number in N = 32768, W = 512, B = 512 and you must move this kernel across the ridge to the memory roof. Which number, which direction, and roughly how far? Commit to an answer — the widget above will grade it, and getting it wrong here is cheaper than getting it wrong in a tuning sweep.

It is B, downwards, and it takes two halvings. Holding N = 32768, W = 512 and varying only the block:

Live-block counts per query head, per layer. Check them against the reference sheet you wrote in session 1 — 1,270 / 381 / 127 are its numbers, unchanged. The K DMAs column is what session 1 could not see.

Read the last two columns together and something uncomfortable falls out. The lowest floor in the table is at B = 256, and it is on the memory side of the ridge. The configuration you have been carrying since lesson 04 is not the fastest one available, and the fastest one available is not compute-bound.

The tension, resolved

Session 1's sheet says smaller blocks are better: overhead is 1 + B/W, so B = 128 wastes 1.25× the necessary pairs against 2.00× at B = 512. Lesson 05 says smaller blocks are worse: below W the kv index sequence stops rising and copy elision collapses — look at the K DMA column, 1,269 fetches for 1,270 live blocks, one per block, elision gone entirely. Both are right. Shrinking B buys FLOPs and sells intensity, and the roofline is the only instrument in this course that prices both sides of that trade in the same unit.

6 · Decode, in one line

CONTEXT.md has asserted since before lesson 01 that prefill is compute-bound and decode is bandwidth-bound. Here is the arithmetic it was standing on. One decode step, one local layer: one query row against W cached keys.

FLOPs = 4 · W · H · D   ·   bytes ≈ 2 · W · D · 2   ⟹   intensity = H

W cancels. D cancels. What is left is H — the number of query heads sharing one KV head, which for Gemma 3 1B's MQA is 4. Measured with Q and O folded in: 3.97 FLOPs/byte, against a ridge of 240. Decode is memory-bound by 60×, and no window size, no head dimension and no block size changes that.

Why this is the whole argument for MQA

Every query head you attach to a KV head is one more use of bytes you already paid for, so intensity is the sharing ratio. Gemma 3 1B's 4 would have to become ~240 to reach the ridge, which is why nobody tries: MQA is not trying to make decode compute-bound, it is trying to make decode's bytes fewer. Same reason the window exists. And it is why the kernel in this repo is a prefill kernel — prefill is where an MXU can be saturated at all.

7 · The boundary of the prediction

You have no chip. Everything above is a floor, computed from counts, and a floor is not a forecast. State the boundary before someone else does:

  • 692 µs is a lower bound, not an estimate. It says no schedule doing these FLOPs on one v5e finishes sooner. It does not say this kernel finishes then, and real attention kernels land well below peak.
  • The compute roof assumes all four MXUs fed with 128-wide tiles, every cycle, with no pipeline bubbles. That is the one assumption in the model most likely to be false, and it is not visible on the chart.
  • The DMA counts are interpret-mode counts. They come from lesson 03's documented elision rule applied to a step sequence, verified under two CPU interpreters. Whether Mosaic honours that rule on the hardware path is still an open gap — and if it does not, every byte count here is a floor too.
  • The ridge is 229–241, not 240. Three published bandwidths, 4.9% apart. It changes no conclusion in this lesson, which is a thing you can only say after checking.
  • The score tile is f32 and is not in the byte count, because it never leaves VMEM. It is in lesson 04's budget instead. Two different limits, two different inventories — do not merge them.

8 · Retrieve it

Two of these need a number you have to work out rather than recall.

Read this next

Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model — Williams, Waterman & Patterson, Berkeley UCB/EECS-2008-134, 2008. The origin of everything above, and short. Read it for the ceilings it adds below each roof — the ones for imperfect ILP, missing SIMD and unit stride, which are exactly the “all four MXUs fed” assumption made visible. Then FlashAttention §3.2, which is lesson 01's reading seen from this side: its IO-complexity result is an argument about arithmetic intensity conducted without the word.

Where this goes

That is the last of the five things MISSION.md asks for that can be learned without writing the kernel. The sixth — explaining the remaining gap to splash_attention in mechanistic terms — cannot, because a gap needs two things to measure between, and src/ still has no kernel in it. Every ingredient now exists: the predicate, the recurrence, the axis order, the block budget, the tables, and a floor to check the result against. The next session should write the file.
