Lesson 02 · Windowed prefill

The normaliser you don't have yet

Softmax needs a denominator over every score. A flash kernel only ever holds one tile of them. The fix is not an approximation — it is an accumulator you are allowed to correct after the fact.

Lesson 01 ended with a kernel that knows which tiles to load. Now it has to do something with one. You load a tile of keys, compute its slice of QKT, and immediately hit a wall: softmax needs a sum over all the scores in the row, and you are holding two per cent of them.

The obvious escape is two passes — sweep once to find the denominator, sweep again to use it. Look at what that costs. A second sweep means loading every live KV tile twice, and lesson 01 was an entire lesson about the fact that loading KV tiles is the thing you are minimising. Two-pass softmax hands back the win you just spent a lesson earning.

So: one pass. Which means committing to a denominator before you have seen the data that determines it.

1 · Four numbers, by hand

One query row, four keys, two tiles of two. Small enough that you can check every figure below on paper — and you should, because the general form in §2 is worth nothing if this part is a black box.

Scores [1, 3] arrive first, then [5, 2]. Do what safe softmax does — subtract the max — except the only max you have is the max so far.

Tile 0. Running max m = 3. Accumulate:

ℓ0 = e1−3 + e3−3 = 0.135335 + 1 = 1.135335

Tile 1 arrives, and it contains a 5. The running max jumps to m = 5, and every term in ℓ0 is now based on the wrong one. You do not have those scores any more — the tile is gone. What you do have is a single number, and a single number is enough:

e3−5 · (e1−3 + e3−3)  =  e1−5 + e3−5

Read that line twice, because it is the entire lesson. Multiplying by emold−mnew distributes across the sum and the old max cancels out of every term at once. The accumulator is not corrected approximately, or on average. It is rebased exactly, at a cost of one multiply, whatever it happened to contain.

So finish the pass:

ℓ1 = 0.135335 · 1.135335 + e5−5 + e2−5
= 0.153651 + 1 + 0.049787 = 1.203438

And the two-pass answer, computed the ordinary way with the true max in hand: e1−5 + e3−5 + e5−5 + e2−5 = 0.018316 + 0.135335 + 1 + 0.049787 = 1.203438. Identical.

Verified, not asserted

Every figure on this page was checked in float64 and float32 against numpy. The two denominators above agree to the last bit. One honest caveat: that is this example being kind. The rebasing is exact in exact arithmetic — in floating point it carries the same rounding any summation does, so online softmax is as accurate as safe softmax, not more. The widget in §4 reports which steps land bit-exact and which land a ulp away.

2 · The recurrence

Now generalise what you just did. Tiles of scores S1 … ST for one query row, with m0 = −∞ and ℓ0 = 0:

mj = max(mj−1, rowmax Sj)
αj = emj−1−mj
ℓj = αj · ℓj−1 + Σ eSj−mj

That is three lines and one of them is a max. But the denominator was never the point — you want PV, the second matmul. The output accumulator takes the same α:

Oj = αj · Oj−1 + eSj−mj Vj   ·   answer = OT / ℓT

Oj is a vector of length head_dim = 256; α is a scalar per query row. Rescaling the output is a scalar–vector multiply, not another matmul — which is why the whole scheme is affordable.

This is the step people drop. It is not enough to fix the denominator; the numerator accumulated under the old max too, and it is rebased by exactly the same factor. Divide once, at the very end.

3 · The invariant that makes it checkable

Here is the property to hold onto, because it is what you assert in a test when you write this kernel for real:

ℓj = Σseen so far es − mj   and   Oj / ℓj = exact softmax attention over tiles 1…j

Every intermediate state is a correct answer to a smaller problem. Not a partial result that only becomes meaningful at the end — a finished one, for the data consumed so far. That is why a single pass is sufficient, and it is a far better debugging handle than the final answer: a kernel that is wrong at tile 3 can be caught at tile 3.

4 · Why the max, though?

Now the question your mission actually names — why is the running max required rather than merely convenient? The usual answer, "numerical stability", is true and too vague to act on. Take it apart, because there are two separate pressures and they want different things.

First: the correction has nothing to do with the max. Go back to §1 and look at what made the rebasing work — ea−b distributing over a sum. That identity holds for any sequence of mj you like. Freeze m at zero, freeze it at tile 0's max, set it from a coin flip: the recurrence still computes the exact softmax. The invariant in §3 never breaks. So exactness is not what the max is buying, and if you thought it was, that is the misconception to drop here.

Second: two constraints do the real work.

  • "Running" is forced by the single pass. The global max is not available — that is the entire premise. Whatever you subtract has to be computable from the tiles you have already seen.
  • "Max" is forced by floating point. Subtract the running max and every exponent is ≤ 0, so every term lands in (0, 1] — no overflow is possible, ever, by construction. Not "unlikely on typical inputs". Not possible.

That guarantee is worth pricing. exp overflows float32 above ln(3.4028×1038) = 88.72; a score of 89 is inf, the row sum is inf, and every probability comes back NaN. One score, anywhere in a 32K-long row, and the row is gone.

Verified with ml_dtypes: bfloat16 tops out at 3.3895×1038, ln = 88.72 — the same ceiling as float32, since bf16 trades mantissa bits (7 vs 23) for float32's exponent range. float16 would be a different story entirely: ln(65504) = 11.09, and a score of 12 overflows.

And one more property, easy to miss and load-bearing: mj is non-decreasing, so mj−1 − mj ≤ 0 and therefore αj ∈ (0, 1] always. The rescale can only ever shrink the accumulator. A correction factor that could exceed 1 would amplify whatever error was already in there, once per tile, for every tile in the row. This one cannot. That is the difference between a recurrence that is stable over hundreds of steps and one that merely works on your test case.

The win

"Running" comes from the algorithm; "max" comes from the hardware. The recurrence would be exact without the max, and useless. The max makes it representable — bounded exponents, and a correction factor that never amplifies. That is the answer to "why required rather than convenient", and it is the shape of argument you will make again for every accumulator you ever put in a kernel.

5 · Break it yourself

Set subtract to subtract nothing or freeze m at tile 0 and watch the invariant column: it keeps holding, exactly as §4 claims, right up until the floats give out. Try the spike preset in float32 — then switch to float64 and watch the same recurrence survive. Nothing about the algorithm changed; only the range did.

6 · Retrieve it

Derive rather than recognise. Question 5 reaches back to lesson 01 — that mixing is deliberate.

Read this next

Online normalizer calculation for softmax (Milakov & Gimelshein, NVIDIA, 2018) — §3, and Algorithm 3. Four pages, and it is the origin of the recurrence above: dj ← dj−1·emj−1−mj + exj−mj, proved correct by induction. Read the induction — it is the formal version of the invariant in §3. FlashAttention inherits this wholesale; this is where it comes from.

Correction to lesson 01

Lesson 01's footer had FlashAttention's sections the wrong way round. In arXiv:2205.14135, §3.1 is "An Efficient Attention Algorithm With Tiling and Recomputation" — the tiling and the online-softmax recurrence, as Algorithm 1 with m and ℓ. §3.2 is "Analysis: IO Complexity". So §3.1 is this lesson's reading, and §3.2 is the memory-traffic argument that lesson 01 should have pointed at. Lesson 01 has been amended.
