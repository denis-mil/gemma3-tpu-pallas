Lesson 04 · Windowed prefill

Two rules your CPU won't enforce

Lesson 03 took (8, 128) on faith and never asked whether the blocks fit. Both debts come due here — and both are checks that your only available machine will silently decline to make.

You now know how to decide which tiles are worth loading, what to do with one when it lands, and which grid axis keeps an operand resident. All three are decisions about the block. None of them says how big the block may be.

Two constraints answer that, and they have nothing in common except their failure mode:

That third column is why this is one lesson and not two. Your workflow is develop on CPU under interpret mode, validate on scarce Colab TPU time. Both of these rules are invisible from the first half of it, so both have to be applied by hand, at authoring time, by you. What follows is how.

1 · Rule one: eight and one hundred and twenty-eight

On TPU, only blocks with rank at least 1 are supported. Furthermore, the last two dimensions of your block shape must be equal to the respective dimension of the overall array, or be divisible by 8 and 128 respectively. For blocks of rank 1, the block dimension must be equal to the array dimension, or be a multiple of 1024, or be a power of 2 and at least 128 * (32 / bitwidth(dtype)). — Grids and BlockSpecs

Two things about that sentence before we use it. It is a disjunction — a block dimension may satisfy the rule by being divisible, or by simply being the whole array along that axis. And it constrains only the last two axes. Leading axes are free.

The reason is not arbitrary, and the docs give it as hardware, not convention:

TPUs perform the bulk of the computation on 2D vector registers, which are typically of size 8x128 for 32-bit values… Pallas will only ever consider mapping the last two dimensions of intermediate arrays to the 8x128 vector register dimensions (sublanes and lanes respectively). — Writing TPU kernels with Pallas · Array Layouts

Confirmed on this machine rather than taken from the page: get_tpu_info_for_chip(TPU_V5E, 1) reports num_sublanes = 8, num_lanes = 128. The same call is what generates the published hardware reference table, so the page and the source cannot disagree.

So 8 × 128 is the shape of a register. A block whose trailing dimensions do not line up with it is not rejected for tidiness — it cannot be laid into registers without waste. And the waste is quantified:

all vector computation is padded up to the tile size. Adding a two 1x1 arrays costs as much as adding two 8x128 arrays, and adding two 8x128x1x1 arrays will be 1024 times as expensive as adding two 8x128 arrays, since the 8x128x1x1 array will be padded to 8x128x8x128. — same section
The win

The lane dimension is 128 whether you use it or not. That single sentence explains a design smell and a design pattern at once. The smell: a trailing dimension of size 1 costs a full tile, so (bq, 1) and (bq, 128) occupy the same registers — one of them is free width you threw away. The pattern: this is why splash_attention broadcasts its rank-1 arrays out to the lane count instead of carrying them narrow. You will see the receipt for that in §6.

2 · Apply it by hand, because nothing else will

Here is the part that matters for how you work. An illegal block shape runs perfectly well on your laptop: 

block_shape=(3, 7) on a (6, 7) array
  interpret=True        -> ACCEPTED   shape (6, 7)
  InterpretParams()     -> ACCEPTED   shape (6, 7)

3 is neither equal to the array's 6 nor divisible by 8, so the second-minor axis is illegal. Both interpreters run it and hand back correct numbers.

Checked in both modes, deliberately

Research 0001 recorded this result under interpret=True only. Session 5 found a claim in that same document that was true in one interpreter and false in the other, generalised to "interpret mode" — so this one was re-run in both before being taught. It holds in both, and so does the padding behaviour in §3. The generalisation is safe here. It was not safe last time, and the difference is that somebody checked.

So block-shape legality is a hardware-only check, and a clean interpret-mode run is evidence of nothing whatsoever about it. Apply the rule yourself. This does that:

The presets walk the interesting cases: an illegal shape that runs anyway, a whole-array block that is legal precisely because it is whole, a legal Gemma-shaped query tile, and the one case where the rule as written does not decide.

An ambiguity, flagged rather than resolved

"must be equal to the respective dimension of the overall array, or be divisible by 8 and 128 respectively" has two readings. Per-axis: each trailing axis independently either matches the array or divides. Paired: either both match, or both divide. They agree almost everywhere and disagree exactly when one axis is satisfied by equality and the other by divisibility — an (8, 7) block on a (16, 7) array is legal under the first reading and illegal under the second. The docs do not disambiguate, and no CPU can tell you. The checker reports it as ambiguous rather than guessing; treat it as illegal until a chip says otherwise. This is a good candidate for your first jax-ml/jax Discussions post — it is small, concrete, and genuinely undecided by the documentation.

3 · When the block does not divide the array

Legality is not the same question as divisibility. A block may be perfectly legal and still not tile the array evenly — bkv = 128 against a kv length of 500, say. Pallas permits it, and defines exactly what you get:

If the block shape does not divide evenly the overall shape then the last iteration on each axis will still receive references to blocks of block_shape but the elements that are out-of-bounds are padded on input and discarded on output. The values of the padding are unspecified, and you should assume they are garbage. In the interpret=True mode, we pad with NaN for floating-point values… but this behavior should not be depended upon. — Grids and BlockSpecs

Verified here, in both modes, on a (7, 5) array of ones read through (2, 3) blocks — 1.0 where the block's last element is real, NaN where it is padding: 

[[ 1. nan]
 [ 1. nan]
 [ 1. nan]
 [nan nan]]

Read two consequences off that, both of which cost people kernels:

  • The grid still runs every step. All eight, here. A padded block is not a skipped block — it is fetched, computed, and its out-of-range outputs thrown away. Traffic and FLOPs are paid in full.
  • Padding pads the shape. It does not neutralise the values. Garbage rows go into your QKᵀ like any other rows. Nothing is masked for you.

That second point lands directly on lesson 02. A running max over a tile containing unspecified garbage is a running max over garbage, and one +inf in the padding poisons the whole query row through α. The mask is not optional decoration on top of a tiled kernel; it is what makes the tiling survivable.

4 · Rule two: the VMEM budget

Now the other question lesson 03 skipped. Every operand your kernel touches is a VMEM buffer, and double buffering — the thing that made the pipeline overlap compute with transfer — was never free. It was a factor of two you had not yet been asked to pay.

The default buffer count is 2 for all inputs and outputs. — TPU Pipelining · Multiple Buffering

Which gives the whole rule, in one line per operand:

bytes(operand) = prod(block_shape) × itemsize × buffer_count

with three amendments, each of which moves real numbers:

  • Scratch counts once. Buffers from scratch_shapes "are persistent across kernel iterations" (TPU Pipelining), so they are not doubled.
  • A whole-array operand counts once too. When a block covers the entire array and its index_map returns literal zeros, lowering forces single buffering — there is no next slice to prefetch. (Research 0001 §2.3, from lowering.py.)
  • The score tile is in the budget. It never appears in a BlockSpec, because it is an intermediate — but details.rst names "space necessary to hold spilled vector registers" as part of what can overflow VMEM. A (512, 512) f32 tile is 256 registers' worth of 8 × 128. It is not going to live in the register file.

Add it up for this repo's shapes — head_dim = 256, MQA so one KV head, sliding_window = 512, inputs bf16 and accumulators f32:

Four megabytes. Three of operands and scratch, one of score tile. Against any plausible v5e ceiling that is not close to binding, and the honest conclusion for this kernel is that the VMEM budget is not the constraint you should be designing around. That is worth knowing precisely because the next kernel may be different, and §6 is how you tell which one you are in.

This table was re-derived, and the source it replaces was wrong

Research 0001 §4.3 carries a version of this table marked inferred — the rules are cited, the multiplication was the research agent's. Re-derived from scratch for this lesson, its total is exactly right and its inventory is not: it has no o_scratch row, and compensates by typing the output f32. Those two errors cancel, because an f32 output at one buffer is the same bytes as a bf16 output at two. Switch the widget's output dtype to f32 and the cancellation breaks: the real figure goes to 3.5 MiB while the old table still says 3. A right answer reached through a wrong inventory is not a right answer — it is one that stops being right the moment a shape changes. Scratch inventory here is taken from splash_attention_kernel.py lines 1099–1101, which declares m_scratch and l_scratch as (bq, 128) f32 and o_scratch as (bq, head_dim) f32.

5 · The number nobody agrees on

You will have noticed the ceiling in that widget is a dropdown. That is not a convenience. JAX contradicts itself about how much VMEM a v5e has, inside its own repository:

details.rst"fairly large… (16MB+)"16 MB

pipelining.md"TPU v5p contains 96MB"— (v5p)
tpu_info.pyvmem_capacity_bytes— (v5p: 64 MiB)
Cloud TPU v5enever mentions VMEM—

Note the fourth row: v5p is quoted as 96 MB on one JAX doc page and 64 MiB in JAX source. That is a flat contradiction inside one repository, which tells you the prose pages are not being maintained against the numbers. details.rst carries no reviewed-date at all, and "16MB+" is exactly the v4 figure.

How to resolve it

Prefer tpu_info.py: 128 MiB. It is machine-readable, versioned with the library, queryable from your own laptop, and it is what generates the published hardware table — the doc page and the source cannot drift apart. Confirmed on this machine: vmem_capacity_bytes = 134217728.

But do not stop at picking a side, because at our shapes the disagreement changes no decision. Solve the budget for the largest square block each ceiling admits: 1419 under 16 MiB, 5075 under 128 MiB. Gemma 3 1B's window is 512. Both ceilings are several times any block you would plausibly choose, so the question is moot here — and saying that is a better answer than picking the bigger number. The disagreement starts mattering when you tune a block into the low thousands, and if you ever do, the number you need is not on this table at all: see the trapdoor below.

The trapdoor under both numbers

Neither figure is what your kernel actually gets. pltpu.CompilerParams documents vmem_limit_bytes and says it "must be used in conjunction with the --xla_tpu_scoped_vmem_limit_kib=N flag" — so the effective budget is an XLA scoped limit, not the physical capacity. The default value of that flag is not stated anywhere in the JAX Python tree. It is an open gap in RESOURCES.md, and it is the number a real out-of-memory error would be measured against.

6 · The shape of the function, not the size of the answer

"4 MiB" is an answer about Gemma 3 1B. The mission wants the thing that transfers. Look at the widget's two colours again, and change bq:

The win

A tiled attention kernel has two VMEM regimes, and the crossover is a number you can compute before writing any code. Below it, the buffers dominate and doubling the block roughly doubles the footprint — so the ×2 from double buffering is the term worth arguing about. Above it, the score tile dominates and doubling the block quadruples it — so bq × bkv is the term worth arguing about, and decoupling the pipeline block from the compute block starts to pay. For these shapes the crossover is b = 1536, and the window is 512, so we are firmly in the linear regime. Which regime am I in is the question that transfers to the next kernel. "4 MiB" is not.

splash_attention does exactly this decoupling: bkv and bkv_compute are separate knobs, with bkv % bkv_compute == 0 enforced. Pipeline granularity and compute granularity are not the same choice. Phase 6 material.

And now §1's loose thread. m and ℓ from lesson 02 are one scalar per query row — logically (bq,). splash_attention declares them (bq, 128), one hundred and twenty-eight times as many elements. That is not waste. The lane dimension pads to 128 either way, so the narrow version occupies the same tiles and buys nothing; the wide one at least has somewhere to put per-lane partial work. In the budget above, m_scratch and l_scratch cost 256 KiB each — and a (bq, 1) version would have cost the same.

Where this goes

You can now write a complete BlockSpec and defend every number in it: which axis goes last, how big the block may be, and whether it fits. What the grid still cannot do is refuse a block. It walks every kv tile, including the ones lesson 01 proved were dead. Phase 5 is where the mask predicate finally becomes an index_map — and the mechanism turns out not to be new machinery at all, but lesson 03's copy elision driven by a prefetched table.
